## First, the page it goes to

Would a reader of that sentence plausibly want to go on to that page, and be glad they did? It needn't be on the same topic as the whole page: a related page that takes the thought further is a good link, such as a post on why seedheads are left standing from a passage about meadows, or the services page from a passage about what the work involves. It is wrong when it would mislead: the page only shares a word with the sentence ("winter pruning" linked to winter opening hours), promises something the sentence isn't about, or a reader would feel sent somewhere they didn't ask to go.

Drop a link only for this: when the page is wrong or misleading there.

## Then, the words

When the page is right, look at the marked words. Good link words say where the link goes and make sense on their own: "leave the seedheads standing", "our planting plans", "tell us about your garden". Weak ones are vague ("here", "read more", "this page"), a fragment that only makes sense with the rest of the sentence, a single common word, or words that sit oddly in the sentence.

When the words are weak, don't drop the link: give better ones in `anchor`.

- Two to eight words, copied **exactly** from the **same sentence**, letter for letter: you choose a different stretch of the sentence, you never change a word.
- Words that describe the page the link goes to, as a reader would expect from them.
- Not in a heading, bold text, a quotation or an existing link, not across two sentences, and not the page's own title.

If no stretch of the sentence would do better, keep the link as it is.
